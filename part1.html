🦠 Mission 1: Meet the Enemy

Ana just sneezed. Let's find out what a "germ" actually is, and how it travels from person to person.
About 35 min
🎬 Watch: Viruses (Updated) — The Amoeba SistersA quick, clear look at what a virus actually is and how it hijacks a host cell to make copies of itself.

What is a pathogen?

A pathogen is a microorganism that causes disease. Not every microorganism is a pathogen — most of the microbes around you (and even inside you) are harmless, or even helpful. But the ones that do cause illness fall into four main groups, and each one causes disease in a slightly different way.

🦠 Bacteria

Single living cells, far smaller than your own cells. Some make you ill by releasing toxins. Example: Salmonella (food poisoning).

🧬 Viruses

Not even cells — tiny particles that can only reproduce by hijacking one of your own cells and forcing it to make copies. Example: influenza (the flu).

🍄 Fungi

Simple organisms that can grow on or in the body, especially skin. Example: Tinea, which causes athlete's foot and ringworm.

🦟 Protoctists

Single-celled organisms, sometimes spread by an insect. Example: Plasmodium, carried by mosquitoes, which causes malaria.

Common mistake Thinking "germ" always means bacteria. Bacteria, viruses, fungi, and protoctists are all completely different kinds of organisms — which is exactly why one single medicine can't treat all of them. Antibiotics, for instance, only work on bacteria (more on this in Mission 5).

How do pathogens spread?

A pathogen needs a way to travel from one host to the next. This is called transmission, and it usually happens in one of four ways:

RouteHow it worksExample
Direct contactTouching skin, blood, or other body fluids of an infected personSkin infections, some STIs
Droplet infectionTiny droplets released by a cough or sneeze, breathed in by someone nearbyColds, flu
Contaminated food or waterSwallowing a pathogen that has gotten into food or drinkCholera, food poisoning
A vectorAn organism (often an insect) carries the pathogen from one host to another, without getting sick itselfMalaria, carried by mosquitoes
Vectors aren't victims A mosquito that carries malaria doesn't get malaria itself — it just picks up the Plasmodium pathogen from one person's blood and delivers it to the next person it bites. That's exactly what makes something a "vector": a carrier, not a victim.
💭 Think about it: You have a cold and sneeze into your hand, then shake a friend's hand a minute later. How might the virus reach your friend?

Droplets from your sneeze land on your hand. If your friend then touches your hand and later touches their own nose, mouth, or eyes, the virus can transfer to them too — so a sneeze that starts as "droplet infection" can finish as "direct contact." This is exactly why covering a sneeze with your elbow (not your hand) and washing your hands often both matter.

Virus

Do

  • Wash your hands, especially before eating
  • Cover coughs and sneezes with your elbow
  • Cook and store food properly
  • Use insect repellent in mosquito-prone areas

Don't

  • Share drinking bottles or cutlery when someone is unwell
  • Assume every pathogen spreads the same way
  • Drink water you're not sure is clean
  • Touch your face right after touching shared surfaces

🛡️ Mission 2: The Body's Walls

Before Mac or Ana even get involved, most pathogens have already been stopped cold. Here's how.
About 30 min
🛡️ Nothing gets past the walls

Your first line of defense

Long before a pathogen ever meets a white blood cell, it has to get past a set of barriers — physical walls and chemical traps working together. Scientists call this the first line of defense, and most of the time, it's enough on its own.

🧴 Skin

Unbroken skin is a tough, physical wall most pathogens simply can't get through. Sweat is also slightly acidic, which makes it harder for bacteria to grow there.

💧 Mucus & mucous membranes

The lining of your nose, throat, and airways produces sticky mucus that traps pathogens and dust before they get any further in.

🌬️ Cilia

Tiny hair-like structures lining your airways constantly sweep that trapped, germ-filled mucus upward, out of your lungs — a bit like a conveyor belt, sometimes nicknamed the "mucus escalator."

🧪 Stomach acid

Your stomach produces hydrochloric acid strong enough to kill most pathogens that arrive in food or drink, before they can cause harm.

😢 Tears & saliva

Both contain an enzyme called lysozyme, which breaks down the cell walls of bacteria on contact.

🩹 Blood clotting

A cut breaks the skin barrier — but platelets and a protein called fibrin quickly form a clot, sealing the wound and blocking the entry point.

Common mistake Thinking a barrier only has to be physical. Stomach acid and lysozyme never touch a pathogen with a "wall" at all — they destroy it chemically. Physical and chemical barriers work as a team.
💭 Think about it: Why might smoking make someone more likely to catch chest infections?

Smoke damages the cilia lining the airways, so they sweep mucus (and the pathogens trapped in it) out much less effectively. With that barrier weakened, pathogens are more likely to reach deeper into the lungs and cause an infection.

💭 Think about it: Why does a doctor tell you to keep a cut covered with a plaster while it heals?

A scab is still forming a complete seal underneath. Covering the cut keeps dirt and pathogens away from the wound while the clot finishes sealing it, so nothing can sneak in through the gap before your skin barrier is fully rebuilt.

Barrier
Something that physically or chemically stops a pathogen getting in.
Mucous membrane
A sticky lining, like inside the nose, that traps pathogens.
Cilia
Tiny hair-like structures that sweep mucus (and trapped germs) out of the airways.
Lysozyme

White blood cells: your internal army

If a pathogen gets past the skin and barriers, it meets your white blood cells — cells that travel around in your blood specifically to fight infection. There are two main kinds: phagocytes, which Mac belongs to, and lymphocytes, which is Ana's family (you'll meet them properly in Mission 4). This mission is all about Mac's job.

Phagocytosis, step by step

Phagocytes defend the body through a process called phagocytosis — engulfing and destroying pathogens. It happens in three stages:

1️⃣ Detect

Damaged cells and pathogens release chemical signals. Phagocytes sense these and move straight toward the source.

2️⃣ Engulf

The phagocyte flows around the pathogen and takes it completely inside itself, trapping it in an internal pocket.

3️⃣ Digest

Powerful enzymes inside the phagocyte break the pathogen down completely, destroying it for good.

⚡ Non-specific

Phagocytes attack any pathogen the exact same way — they don't need to recognize it first. That's what makes them so fast.

What is pus, really? That thick, yellowish fluid around an infected wound isn't a sign something has gone extra wrong — it's mostly a mix of dead phagocytes, destroyed pathogens, and tissue fluid. In a strange way, pus is a battle already being won.
Common mistake Thinking redness and swelling around a wound (inflammation) is only a bad sign. It's often the opposite — it means extra blood, carrying extra phagocytes, is being rushed straight to the site of infection.
💭 Think about it: If phagocytes can attack almost any pathogen, why does the body still need a more specific kind of defense too?

Phagocytes are fast but generic — they treat every pathogen the same way and don't "remember" it afterward. Some pathogens are also good at avoiding them. A second, more targeted defense — precise enough to recognize one exact pathogen and remember it for next time — catches what phagocytes alone might miss. That's exactly what Ana's lymphocytes do, coming up in Mission 4.

Do

  • Clean a cut so phagocytes aren't overwhelmed
  • Get enough sleep — it helps your immune cells work well
  • Eat a balanced diet to support your white blood cells

Don't

  • Ignore a wound that stays red, hot, or swollen for days
  • Assume phagocytes alone can handle every serious infection
  • Pick at a scab before it's ready — you'll reopen the entry point
