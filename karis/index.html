Day 1 · Clinical Briefing

What You're Actually Diagnosing

You can't triage a patient correctly if you don't understand what's making them sick. This briefing covers the full picture: the four kinds of pathogen, how the body already fights them, and why an antibiotic is the right call for some patients and useless for others.

Why triage decisions start in the body

Every decision you make on shift is really just supporting a system that's already running: the patient's own immune system. Long before any medicine is given, the body is already fighting using layers of defense that build on each other. A medic who understands exactly what those layers are doing — and exactly where a medicine like an antibiotic fits in — makes far better calls than one just guessing from symptoms alone.

1The four kinds of patient you'll see

A pathogen is a microorganism that causes disease, and it always falls into one of four groups. Telling them apart correctly is the single most important skill on your shift, because your treatment options are completely different for each.

🦠 Bacteria

Single-celled living organisms. Some release toxins. Example: infected wounds, Salmonella food poisoning. Antibiotics work here.

🧬 Viruses

Not even cells — hijack a host cell to reproduce. Example: colds, flu. Antibiotics do nothing here.

🍄 Fungi

Simple organisms growing on or in the body. Example: ringworm, athlete's foot. Needs an antifungal, not an antibiotic.

🦟 Protoctists

Single-celled, often vector-spread. Example: malaria, via mosquito. Needs an antimalarial, not an antibiotic.

Pathogens reach a patient by direct contact, droplet infection, contaminated food or water, or a vector — an organism, often an insect, that carries a pathogen between hosts without getting sick itself. A patient's history of exposure is often your first clue to which of the four you're dealing with.

2Line one: barriers (working before the patient even walks in)

Before any white blood cell or medicine gets involved, the body's own barriers are already at work: skin as a physical wall, mucus and cilia trapping and sweeping pathogens out of the airways, stomach acid destroying most swallowed pathogens, lysozyme in tears and saliva breaking down bacterial cell walls, and blood clotting sealing a wound before pathogens can enter through it. A broken barrier — a cut, a burn — is exactly why a wound infection needs different handling than an infection that started elsewhere.

3Line two: phagocytes (the body's own first responders)

White blood cells are the body's infection-fighters, and phagocytes respond first. Phagocytosis happens in three stages: detect, engulf, digest. Phagocytes are non-specific — fast, but generic. That thick, yellowish pus you'll see on patient charts is mostly dead phagocytes and destroyed pathogens: visible proof the body is already fighting, with or without your help.

4Line three: lymphocytes and antibodies (why one infection rarely repeats)

Every pathogen carries antigens — surface markers that identify it as foreign. Lymphocytes recognize one specific antigen and produce antibodies: Y-shaped proteins shaped to match that antigen exactly. This response is specific, unlike a phagocyte's general attack. Afterward, some lymphocytes remain as memory cells, ready to react instantly if that exact pathogen returns — which is why a patient rarely catches the same viral illness (like chickenpox) twice.

🔑 A perfect match, every time
Different response, same patient's body — this is what backs up your phagocytes once they're overwhelmed.

5Why the antibiotic decision is the one that actually matters

There are two routes to immunity. Active immunity is the body producing its own antibodies, naturally or from a vaccine. Passive immunity is receiving ready-made antibodies from elsewhere — fast, but temporary. Neither one is what you're deciding on shift; you're deciding whether to spend a scarce antibiotic dose.

Antibiotics kill or stop bacteria — and only bacteria. They do nothing to a virus, a fungus, or a protoctist, because antibiotics target structures that only bacterial cells have. Giving one to the wrong patient doesn't just fail to help them — it wastes a dose a genuinely bacterial patient down the line will need, and contributes to antibiotic resistance: bacteria evolving to survive antibiotics that used to work, one of the most serious problems in real medicine today.

History: how we learned to tell these apart

1796
Edward Jenner creates the world's first vaccine after noticing milkmaids with cowpox rarely caught smallpox.
1876–1884
Robert Koch establishes that specific bacteria cause specific diseases, giving medicine a real way to diagnose infections by cause, not just symptom.
1882
Élie Metchnikoff discovers phagocytosis, later sharing the 1908 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.
1890–1901
Emil von Behring develops diphtheria antitoxin and receives the first-ever Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1901.
1928
Alexander Fleming discovers penicillin — the first antibiotic, and the moment "bacterial vs. viral" became a real treatment decision, not just a diagnosis.
1980
The WHO declares smallpox eradicated worldwide, through vaccination alone.
2020s
The WHO's AWaRe framework becomes a standard clinical tool worldwide, classifying antibiotics to help medics make exactly the kind of call you're about to make.

Real-world application: this is a real job

Every hospital and clinic follows antibiotic stewardship guidelines (like the WHO's AWaRe classification) specifically to stop antibiotics being given when they won't help. Real triage systems — used in emergency rooms worldwide — sort patients by urgency and treatability using exactly this kind of rapid, information-limited decision-making. Rapid diagnostic tests (checking for bacterial vs. viral markers in minutes) are a real, growing tool designed to make the call you're about to make more accurate under real time pressure.

    Day 3 · Handing Off the Shift

    Present Your Phagocyte

    Your opening move

    Hold up your model without naming any parts yet, and ask the room: "What moment do you think this is capturing?" Let a few guesses land before you walk through it.

    What to include

    • Every labeled part, and what it actually does
    • Why your model shows mid-engulfment specifically, not before or after
    • The material-justification decision you're least sure about — and why you still made that call
    • Why the body still needs the slower, specific response (antibodies) even with phagocytes on patrol
    Facilitator only

    If a part of the model looks rough or a material choice feels like a stretch, steer the room's attention there first, not away from it. Real medics are judged by how clearly they can explain an imperfect call, not by pretending every resource was ideal. A kid who can defend WHY a material was the best available option is demonstrating the target skill (decision-making under real constraints) far better than one who happened to have perfect materials on hand.
