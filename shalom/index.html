Day 1 · Briefing

Know Your Enemy Before You Deploy

You can't allocate resources well if you don't understand what you're actually defending against. This briefing covers the full picture: what you're up against, how the body already fights it, and how real health authorities make the same kind of call you're about to make.

Why command decisions start in the body

Every containment decision you'll ever make is really just backing up a system that's already running: the human immune system. Long before any health authority steps in, every person's body is already fighting pathogens on its own, using layers of defense that build on each other. Understanding exactly how those layers work — where they're strong, and where they need backup — is the entire foundation of good outbreak command. A commander who doesn't understand the body's own defenses is making decisions blind.

1. The threat: what you're actually defending against

A pathogen is a microorganism that causes disease. Not all microorganisms are pathogens — but the ones that are fall into four groups, and each one behaves differently enough that your response has to account for the difference.

🦠 Bacteria

Single-celled living organisms. Some cause illness by releasing toxins. Example: Salmonella (food poisoning), Mycobacterium tuberculosis (TB).

🧬 Viruses

Not even cells — tiny particles that hijack a host cell to reproduce. Example: influenza, measles.

🍄 Fungi

Simple organisms that grow on or in the body. Example: Tinea (ringworm, athlete's foot).

🦟 Protoctists

Single-celled organisms, often vector-spread. Example: Plasmodium (malaria), carried by mosquitoes.

Transmission matters just as much as pathogen type. A pathogen can spread by direct contact (touching skin or body fluids), droplet infection (a cough or sneeze), contaminated food or water, or a vector — an organism, often an insect, that carries the pathogen between hosts without getting sick itself. Your containment plan for a droplet-spread disease looks nothing like your plan for a vector-spread one.

2. Line one: barriers (the defense that's already deployed)

Before a single white blood cell gets involved, the body has physical and chemical barriers doing constant, unpaid work: skin as a physical wall, mucus and cilia trapping and sweeping pathogens out of the airways, stomach acid destroying most swallowed pathogens, lysozyme in tears and saliva breaking down bacterial cell walls, and blood clotting sealing a wound before pathogens can use it as an entry point.

🔍 Compare Two Cases

Case A: A settlement's water supply becomes contaminated after flooding damages the filtration system.
Case B: A settlement reports a rise in chest infections after a wildfire fills the air with smoke for a week.

Which body barrier is most directly compromised in each case — and why would a health authority respond differently to the two?

3. Line two: phagocytes (the fast, general-purpose response)

White blood cells are the body's dedicated infection-fighters, and phagocytes are the first kind to respond. Phagocytosis happens in three stages: the phagocyte detects chemical signals from damaged tissue or pathogens, engulfs the pathogen completely, then digests it with enzymes. Phagocytes are non-specific — they attack any pathogen the same way, which makes them fast but generic. That thick, yellowish pus around an infected wound is mostly dead phagocytes and destroyed pathogens — visible evidence a battle already happened.

🧾 Evidence Log

A field report says a settlement's minor wound infections are "healing well on their own, no cause for concern." What specific physical sign, mentioned in this section, would you look for to confirm phagocytes are actually doing their job at the wound site? Name it and explain what it tells you.

4. Line three: lymphocytes and antibodies (the precision strike)

Every pathogen carries antigens — marker molecules on its surface that identify it as foreign. Lymphocytes are white blood cells that recognize one specific antigen; once triggered, they multiply and produce antibodies — Y-shaped proteins shaped to match that one antigen exactly, like a key cut for one lock. Antibodies clump pathogens together and mark them for phagocytes to finish off. This response is specific, unlike a phagocyte's non-specific attack. After the infection clears, some lymphocytes remain as memory cells, ready to trigger a much faster response if that exact pathogen ever returns — the biological basis of immunity.

🎓 Field Training Note

A new recruit on your team doesn't understand why getting chickenpox once usually means you won't get it again. Write the two or three sentences you'd use to explain memory cells to them — simply enough that they could repeat it back correctly to someone else.

5. Vaccines, immunity, and the numbers behind command decisions

There are two routes to immunity. Active immunity is your own body producing antibodies and memory cells — either by catching the disease ("natural") or through a vaccine ("artificial"). Passive immunity is receiving ready-made antibodies from elsewhere (a mother's antibodies crossing to a baby, or an antibody injection) — fast, but temporary, since no memory cells are made. A vaccine exposes the immune system to a safe version of a pathogen's antigens, triggering real antibody and memory-cell production without causing the disease itself.

Here's the number that actually drives outbreak command: every disease has a basic reproduction number (R0) — roughly, how many new people one infected person is likely to infect in a population with no immunity. The more contagious a disease, the higher its R0, and the higher the percentage of a population that needs to be immune before the outbreak can't sustain itself — its herd immunity threshold. This is exactly the number you'll be using in Day 2.

Real-world figures, cited from published epidemiological reviews. The higher a pathogen's R0, the more of a population needs to be immune before its spread naturally stalls out — which is exactly why a "cheap" intervention that works for a low-R0 disease can completely fail against a high-R0 one.

Antibiotics kill or stop bacteria — they do nothing to viruses, since antibiotics target structures bacterial cells have that viruses simply don't. Using antibiotics when they're not needed, or stopping a course early, lets the toughest bacteria survive and multiply — antibiotic resistance, one of the biggest constraints real health authorities plan around today.

History: how we got the tools you're about to use

1796
Edward Jenner notices milkmaids who'd had cowpox rarely caught smallpox, and creates the world's first vaccine.
1879–1885
Louis Pasteur establishes germ theory and develops the first lab-made weakened-pathogen vaccines, including one for rabies.
1882
Élie Metchnikoff discovers phagocytosis, later sharing the 1908 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the work.
1890–1901
Emil von Behring develops diphtheria antitoxin — the first practical use of passive immunity — and receives the first-ever Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1901.
1928
Alexander Fleming discovers penicillin, the first antibiotic, from an accidentally contaminated petri dish.
1980
The World Health Organization officially declares smallpox eradicated — still the only human disease ever eliminated worldwide through vaccination.
2020
mRNA vaccine technology is deployed at unprecedented speed during the COVID-19 pandemic, reshaping how fast a new vaccine program can be launched.

Real-world application: this is a real job

Outbreak command isn't hypothetical. The World Health Organization and national health authorities use R0 and herd immunity thresholds exactly like the table above to decide where to deploy limited vaccine supply first during a real outbreak. National immunization programs (like India's Universal Immunization Programme) run on fixed annual budgets and coverage targets, not unlimited resources. Hospitals follow antibiotic stewardship guidelines (like the WHO's AWaRe classification) specifically to avoid the resistance problem you read about above. International travelers are sometimes required to show a yellow fever vaccination certificate before entering certain countries — a real, everyday example of herd-immunity thinking applied at a border.

    Day 3 · Briefing the Room

    Present Your Containment Cycle

    Your opening move

    Hold up your Region Map so the room can see which zones turned out protected and which didn't. Open with exactly this: "This cycle, I had 12 resources and 6 zones at risk. Here's where they went — and here's what I couldn't cover."

    What to include

    • Which zones you fully protected, and the disease data that justified spending a full vaccination token there
    • At least one zone you deliberately under-resourced — and the reasoning, not just the outcome
    • What the dice revealed that your original plan didn't predict
    • One thing you'd allocate differently if you ran this cycle again
    Facilitator only

    If a zone was lost to spread, steer the room's attention there first, not away from it. Real outbreak commanders almost never contain every zone with a fixed budget — the interesting, defensible part of this briefing is explaining WHY a trade-off led to that loss, not pretending a perfect plan would have prevented it. A kid who can defend an imperfect result is demonstrating more of the target skill (decision-making under real constraints) than one who got lucky with the dice.
